    Other factors and alternative explanations

    Almost every intervention works alongside other things: other programmes, prices, policies, the weather. How far an evaluator has to examine them is the most debated question in CA. Nine short sections, one exercise to try, seven questions and one case to work through.

    On this page
      By the end of this lesson

      You can say how far a given evaluation must examine other factors, place each one you find relative to the causal package, and judge how well it was examined.

      RecallFirst, two questions from Lesson 3

      Answer from memory before reading on.

      01Mayne changed his position, and said so

      Much of the confusion about other factors in CA comes from reading older guidance as current. Mayne set out the change himself (2019, p. 175).

      1. 2011, as Mayne reports it"A contribution claim could be made when external factors were shown not to have contributed significantly."
      2. 2012, as Mayne reports itRival explanations need to be explored.
      3. 2019Both statements were wrong. External factors are usually other causal factors at work, not rivals. Mayne calls the "alternative" and "rival" terms "inappropriate in the context of complex causality".

      His 2019 rule: whether and how the intervention contributed can be explored without examining factors outside the causal package, unless they are causally linked to it. How far to go depends on the question. For "what brought about the impact?", explore the other factors. For "did the intervention contribute, and how?", they need not play a major role (pp. 175 to 176).

      02Other authors give alternative explanations a bigger role

      Lemire, Nielsen and Dybdal (2012) built the Relevant Explanation Finder to identify and weigh influencing factors and alternative explanations. Budhwani and McDavid summarise its criteria: a description of the factor, its type, its level, identifiers that would prove or disprove it, its degree of influence and its implication, with sub-criteria including certainty, robustness, range, prevalence and theoretical grounding (2017, Table 1, p. 12).

      Budhwani and McDavid (2017) describe alternative explanations as standing in for counterfactuals when testing a theory of change (p. 4), and recommend searching for them systematically before collecting data (p. 12).

      Mayne disagreed with both on this point. He notes that Lemire and colleagues concede in a footnote that alternatives are needed only when comparing the relative contribution of the intervention (2019, p. 176).Ton and colleagues add a practical limit: no evaluation can test every alternative explanation, or spend equal effort on each (2019, p. 6).

      03Most other factors are already in your theory of change

      Before looking outside the programme, read its assumptions. Mayne describes a ToC as a model of a causal package: the intervention's activities plus the causal link assumptions that, together, are expected to be sufficient for the intended result. Following Cartwright and Hardie, he calls these assumptions support factors, the events and conditions needed for the intervention to make its contribution (2015, p. 127). The intervention is expected to be the necessary part of that package, the part without which the assumptions alone would not be enough (p. 127).

      Hence, much of what a report later calls "other factors" sits inside the package from the start. Supporting factors often involve other partners doing what the programme needs them to do (Mayne 2012, ILAC Brief 26, p. 2): a ministry releasing funds, a seed company supplying on time, a clinic staying open. Evidence on them is evidence on the ToC, and it belongs in the claim. Mayne ends his conclusions on this point: a model with assumptions as support factors is what connects a ToC to causal packages and contributory causes (p. 139).

      The 2026 quality guidance makes the same point from the other side. Change beyond an intervention's direct influence "always depends on a configuration of actors and factors", and that configuration can vary from one context to another (Ton et al. 2026, p. 3). Hence, the first part of its ToC sets out the factors that drive the change, and only the second part places the intervention among them (pp. 4 to 5). On this view, the factors that drive the change are mapped from the start. The guidance's first part therefore covers what Mayne splits between support factors and external influences.

      04Draw external influences on the diagram, and say what they are

      Mayne defines external influences as "events and conditions unrelated to the intervention" that could also help produce its intended results, for example other programmes with similar aims or wider economic and social change. They sit outside the ToC itself (2015, p. 123). His nutrition examples are a fall in vegetable prices and the industrial fortification of staple foods (p. 123). His generic ToC gives them a box of their own, beside a box for unanticipated results (p. 122).

      Three nuances follow from his account. First, because external influences may be at work, a ToC is a model of a contribution, not of the whole cause; only where none are at work does it model causation (p. 128). Second, an external influence rarely works alone. It works as part of another causal package, which may share some support factors with the intervention's own (p. 128). The same rains can help a seed programme and a neighbouring irrigation scheme at once. Third, a serious negative influence that could undermine the ToC is better written as a causal link assumption, so that it is tracked as a risk rather than left in a side box (p. 128).

      Drawing the box does not commit the evaluator to examining everything in it. As the rest of this lesson shows, how far to go depends on the question. Its value is that readers can see what the analysis knew was there.

      05Where a factor sits decides what you do about it

      Start from Mayne's own distinction between factors inside the causal package and factors outside it (2019, pp. 174 to 175).

      Where it sitsWhat it isWhat to do
      Inside the packageA support factor: a condition captured by a causal link assumption, which had to hold for the intervention to workVerify it as part of the theory of change and the causal narrative
      Outside, linkedAn external factor that acts on the package itselfExamine it, whatever the question
      Outside, not linkedAnother causal factor at work on the outcomeExamine it if the question asks what caused the outcome; otherwise it need not play a major role

      06Place each factor, then change the question

      Case, invented for practice. A school feeding programme in Kotido aims to raise attendance. Place each factor, then switch the evaluation question and watch what you have to do change.

      The evaluation question

      Did the feeding programme contribute to higher attendance, and how?

      The district delivers food to schools on time each term.

      A teachers' strike closes schools for a month, so no meals are served.

      A new road shortens the walk to school for many pupils.

      Place all three, then switch the question.

      07When support factors are weak, the intervention grows

      Many interventions cannot leave their support factors to chance. Much of their effect runs through other organisations whose behaviour they must change (Mayne 2015, pp. 129 to 130). Mayne distinguishes the core intervention from the overall intervention. In agricultural research for development, the core is the research itself, and the overall intervention adds the engagement activities that get the research used (p. 129). The aim of the added activities is to make the whole package sufficient.

      This matters for the claim. When a support factor turns into a programme component, the question "did the intervention contribute?" has two readings: the core activities, or the overall intervention with its engagement work. Say which one the claim is about. A claim about the overall intervention can be stronger and still say little about whether the core activities would have mattered on their own.

      Mayne also notes that interventions play different roles in a package. Some act as a trigger that starts the causal chain, a principal contributory cause. Others join a change process already under way and help it go further or faster (2015, p. 129). Both are contributions. Naming which role the evidence supports is part of the "how" in the two-part claim.

      08Look actively for what the programme did not intend

      Mayne's generic ToC has a box for unanticipated results, positive or, more usually, negative effects of the intervention's own activities and results. Known possibilities should be noted in advance, and after the fact, unanticipated effects "should be actively looked for" (2015, p. 123). His nested nutrition ToC names two: mothers becoming more empowered, and husbands becoming resentful (pp. 127, 132).

      These are not other factors in the sense of this lesson. They are effects the programme itself produced, and they belong in the "how" of the claim. They also feed back into the package. A husband's resentment can undermine the very support factor the behaviour change depends on, which is why the ToC needs room for feedback rather than a one-way chain (p. 123).

      Mayne's 2012 brief adds the practical step. If there were unexpected results, positive or negative, try to understand why they occurred (ILAC Brief 26, p. 3). In practice, this means asking about unintended changes in every round of interviews, not only in a closing question, and recording them alongside the intended ones.

      09Say plainly how strong the evidence on each factor is

      The process tracing logic from Lesson 3 applies here too. Under Collier's scheme, a passed smoking gun substantially weakens rival hypotheses, and only doubly decisive evidence eliminates them (2011, p. 825). Most evidence on other factors is weaker than that. Report what you examined, how, and how strong the evidence was, so a reader can judge for themselves.

      PracticeWork through three factors

      Case, invented for practice A girls' education programme in Arua offers mentoring clubs. Attendance rose. In the same period the government abolished a school fee, a new road made the school easier to reach, and a radio campaign promoted girls' schooling. The evaluation asks whether the clubs contributed to higher attendance, and how. For each factor, say where it sits, where in the pathway it acts, and whether this question requires examining it.

      Fee abolition. Plausibly inside the package as a support factor: families need to afford school for mentoring to matter. Verify it as an assumption of the clubs' pathway.
      New road. Likely outside the package and not linked: another causal factor at work. For this contribution question it need not play a major role, unless club attendance itself depended on the road.
      Radio campaign. It acts on the same attitudes the clubs target, so it is linked at the attitude link. Examine it there, for example by asking girls where they heard the messages.
