    Other factors and alternative explanations

    Almost every intervention works alongside other things: other programmes, prices, policies, the weather. How far an evaluator has to examine them is the most debated question in CA. Five short sections, one exercise to try, five questions and one case to work through.

    On this page
      By the end of this lesson

      You can say how far a given evaluation must examine other factors, place each one you find relative to the causal package, and judge how well it was examined.

      RecallFirst, two questions from Lesson 3

      Answer from memory before reading on.

      1Mayne changed his position, and said so

      Much of the confusion about other factors in CA comes from reading older guidance as current. Mayne set out the change himself (2019, p. 175).

      1. 2011, as Mayne reports itA contribution claim could be made once external factors were shown not to have contributed significantly.
      2. 2012, as Mayne reports itRival explanations need to be explored.
      3. 2019Both statements were incorrect. External factors are usually not rivals but other causal factors at work, and the "alternative" and "rival" labels are inappropriate in complex causality.

      His 2019 rule: whether and how the intervention contributed can be explored without examining factors outside the causal package, unless they are causally linked to it. How far to go depends on the question. For "what brought about the impact?", explore the other factors. For "did the intervention contribute, and how?", they need not play a major role (pp. 175 to 176).

      2Other authors give alternative explanations a bigger role

      Lemire, Nielsen and Dybdal (2012) built the Relevant Explanation Finder to identify and weigh influencing factors and alternative explanations. Budhwani and McDavid summarise its criteria: a description of the factor, its type, its level, identifiers that would prove or disprove it, its degree of influence and its implication, with sub-criteria including certainty, robustness, range, prevalence and theoretical grounding (2017, Table 1, p. 12).

      Budhwani and McDavid (2017) describe alternative explanations as standing in for counterfactuals when testing a theory of change (p. 4), and recommend searching for them systematically before collecting data (p. 12).

      Mayne disagreed with both on this point. He notes that Lemire and colleagues concede in a footnote that alternatives are needed only when comparing the relative contribution of the intervention (2019, p. 176).Ton and colleagues add a practical limit: no evaluation can test every alternative explanation, or spend equal effort on each (2019, p. 6).

      3Where a factor sits decides what you do about it

      Start from Mayne's own distinction between factors inside the causal package and factors outside it (2019, pp. 174 to 175).

      Where it sitsWhat it isWhat to do
      Inside the packageA support factor: a condition captured by a causal link assumption, which had to hold for the intervention to workVerify it as part of the theory of change and the causal narrative
      Outside, linkedAn external factor that acts on the package itselfExamine it, whatever the question
      Outside, not linkedAnother causal factor at work on the outcomeExamine it if the question asks what caused the outcome; otherwise it need not play a major role

      4Place each factor, then change the question

      Case, invented for practice. A school feeding programme in Kotido aims to raise attendance. Place each factor, then switch the evaluation question and watch what you have to do change.

      The evaluation question

      Did the feeding programme contribute to higher attendance, and how?

      The district delivers food to schools on time each term.

      A teachers' strike closes schools for a month, so no meals are served.

      A new road shortens the walk to school for many pupils.

      Place all three, then switch the question.

      5Say plainly how strong the evidence on each factor is

      The process tracing logic from Lesson 3 applies here too. Under Collier's scheme, a passed smoking gun substantially weakens rival hypotheses, and only doubly decisive evidence eliminates them (2011, p. 825). Most evidence on other factors is weaker than that. Report what you examined, how, and how strong the evidence was, so a reader can judge for themselves.

      PracticeWork through three factors

      Case, invented for practice A girls' education programme in Arua offers mentoring clubs. Attendance rose. In the same period the government abolished a school fee, a new road made the school easier to reach, and a radio campaign promoted girls' schooling. The evaluation asks whether the clubs contributed to higher attendance, and how. For each factor, say where it sits, where in the pathway it acts, and whether this question requires examining it.

      Fee abolition. Plausibly inside the package as a support factor: families need to afford school for mentoring to matter. Verify it as an assumption of the clubs' pathway.
      New road. Likely outside the package and not linked: another causal factor at work. For this contribution question it need not play a major role, unless club attendance itself depended on the road.
      Radio campaign. It acts on the same attitudes the clubs target, so it is linked at the attitude link. Examine it there, for example by asking girls where they heard the messages.
