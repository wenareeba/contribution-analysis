Theories of change that can carry a claim

1. Why the theory of change decides everything

In CA the theory of change (ToC) is the outline of the contribution story (Mayne 2019, p. 173). The evidence confirms or fails to confirm it, link by link. So the claim can never be stronger than the ToC underneath it.

Mayne is blunt about this. Using a weak ToC can only lead to weak CA findings (p. 176). And if the ToC has insufficient causal link assumptions, a credible contribution claim is not possible; work built on it is not CA as he describes it (p. 176).

Many programme ToCs were drawn to win funding or to communicate, not to be tested. Mayne notes the evaluator usually has to build or guide a more evaluable version (p. 178).

2. Four terms, used precisely

Mayne (2019, p. 172) defines them this way, and notes that others use them differently, so define your terms in each evaluation:

TermMeaning
Impact pathwayThe sequence of steps linking activities to impact
Theory of changeThe impact pathway plus the causal assumptions behind each link: what has to happen for the link to work
Causal link assumptionAn event or condition necessary, or likely necessary, for a particular link to be realised
ResultsThe outputs, outcomes and impacts of the intervention

The difference between a pathway and a ToC is the assumptions. Boxes and arrows alone are a pathway. They tell you what should happen, not what has to be true for it to happen, so there is nothing for evidence to test at each link.

3. Assumptions are rarely all-or-nothing

Mayne's example (2019, p. 177): a programme teaches mothers about nutritious diets. One result is "mothers adopt new feeding practices". One assumption is "supportive husbands and mother-in-law".

If the in-laws are only somewhat supportive, you get less of the result (some practices adopted), but the assumption is still necessary for that result. If the assumption is not realised at all, there is no result. The fix is to define the result precisely, for example "fully adopting new practices", so it lines up with the assumptions.

Mayne later found a missed assumption in the same example: mothers controlling food distribution in the household. The higher-level version is that the person with power over household food must be reached, who may not be the mother (p. 186).

4. A model for behaviour change: COM-B

Most interventions try to change what some group of people does. Mayne built a generic ToC for this on the COM-B model of Michie, Atkins and West (2014): behaviour changes through the interaction of three necessary elements, capabilities, opportunities and motivation (Mayne 2019, pp. 178 to 179). Because COM-B summarises research on behaviour change, it names the mechanisms at work, which is what a causal narrative needs.

5. Three levels of ToC

There is no single right drawing of a ToC. Mayne (2019, p. 178) uses three, for different jobs:

LevelWhat it showsUse
Narrative ToCA brief account of how the intervention is meant to workCommunication
Overview ToCThe pathways to impact, some steps on each, and the rationale, usually without causal link assumptionsOrientation
Nested ToCsOne per pathway, place or actor group, with explicit causal link assumptionsThe level CA actually tests

Where there are many pathways, a simplified ToC (activities, behaviour change, direct benefits, impact) can cut the data burden. Mayne's advice is to build the detailed nested version first, so you know what the simplified one is suppressing (pp. 180 to 181).

Practice: write the missing assumptions

Case (invented for practice). A school WASH programme in Arua builds handwashing stations next to latrines and trains a school health club. The programme's ToC has one arrow: "Handwashing stations built → pupils wash hands with soap after using the latrine." Write the causal link assumptions this arrow needs. Use COM-B to make sure you have covered all three elements.

Capability. Pupils know when and how to wash hands with soap (taught by the health club).
Opportunity. Water and soap are at the stations every school day; stations are unlocked, close to the latrines, and usable by small children; there is time between lessons.
Motivation. Teachers and club members prompt and model handwashing, and it becomes what pupils expect of each other.
