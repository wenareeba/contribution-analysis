    02 / 08Contribution Analysis

    Theories of change that can carry a claim

    A contribution claim can never be stronger than the theory of change under it. This lesson is about building one that evidence can actually test. Nine short sections, two figures to try, seven questions and one set of assumptions to write.

    On this page
      By the end of this lesson

      You can look at a theory of change and say whether a contribution claim could stand on it, and write the causal link assumptions it is missing.

      01A weak theory of change can only give weak findings

      In CA the theory of change (ToC) is the outline of the contribution story (Mayne 2019, p. 173). Evidence confirms it, or fails to, link by link. So the claim can never be stronger than the ToC underneath it.

      Mayne is blunt about this. A weak ToC can only lead to weak CA findings. And if the ToC has insufficient causal link assumptions, a credible contribution claim is not possible: the work is not CA as he describes it (p. 176).Many programme ToCs were drawn to win funding or to communicate, not to be tested. The evaluator usually has to build or guide a more evaluable version (Mayne 2019, p. 178).

      02The assumptions are what turn a pathway into a theory

      Mayne defines four terms, and notes that others use them differently, so define yours in each evaluation (2019, p. 172).

      TermMeaning
      Impact pathwayThe sequence of steps linking activities to impact
      Theory of changeThe impact pathway plus the causal assumptions behind each link: what has to happen for the link to work
      Causal link assumptionAn event or condition necessary, or likely necessary, for a particular link to be realised
      ResultsThe outputs, outcomes and impacts of the intervention

      Boxes and arrows alone are a pathway. They say what should happen, not what has to be true for it to happen, so there is nothing for evidence to test at each link.

      03The generic model names what has to happen at each step

      Mayne's generic ToC gives each step of the pathway a plain label and attaches a set of assumptions to each link (2015, pp. 122 to 126). He deliberately avoids labels such as immediate, intermediate and final outcomes, which he says have "no inherent meaning" and lead to "wasted debate". What matters is the sequence (p. 139).

      LinkWhat its assumptions are aboutNutrition example
      Outputs to reachThe outputs reach the intended group and are received wellThe mothers reached are the people who decide what children eat
      Reach to capacity changeThe outputs are understood, relevant, culturally acceptable and usefulThe training on nutrition is understood and seen as relevant
      Capacity to behaviour changePeople can afford the change, others accept it, the environment allows itNutritious food is available and affordable; the household supports the change
      Behaviour to direct benefitsThe new practice actually delivers the benefitChildren do not lose other nutritious food when new foods are added
      Direct benefits to well-beingOther conditions needed for well-being are in placeChildren have access to basic health care and sanitation

      Paraphrased from Mayne (2015, pp. 124 to 125). The ToC also shows a timeline, external influences and room for unanticipated results.

      Three points keep the list manageable. Only salient assumptions belong in it, the ones that stand out as relevant to this setting, not every condition in the world (p. 124). Each assumption is also a risk, so the list doubles as a risk register for the ToC (p. 124). And an assumption explains why a link works; it does not restate the link (p. 125). "Mothers change their practices" restates the arrow. "Husbands and mothers-in-law accept the change" explains it.

      04Assumptions are rarely all or nothing

      Mayne's example: a programme teaches mothers about nutritious diets. One result is "mothers adopt new feeding practices". One assumption at that link is "supportive husbands and mother-in-law" (2019, p. 177). Try the three levels of support.

      Support from husband and mother-in-law

      The fix Mayne proposes is to define the result precisely, for example "fully adopting new practices", so that it lines up with the assumptions (p. 177).Mayne later found an assumption he had missed in the same example: mothers controlling food distribution in the household. The higher-level version is that whoever holds power over household food must be reached, and that may not be the mother (2019, p. 186).

      05Behaviour change needs capability, opportunity and motivation

      Most interventions try to change what some group of people does. Mayne built a generic ToC for this on the COM-B model of Michie, Atkins and West (2014): behaviour shifts only when three things are all in place and work together: capabilities, opportunities and motivation (Mayne 2019, pp. 178 to 179). Because COM-B summarises research on behaviour change, it names the mechanisms at work, which is what a causal narrative needs.

      The stages follow Mayne's generic COM-B ToC (2019, Figure 1, p. 179). The two assumptions marked Mayne are his; the others are examples written for this course. External influences act alongside every step.

      06Analyse each link before you test it

      A ToC can be examined before any field data arrive. Mayne calls this a causal link analysis: go through each assumption, judge how likely it is to hold and how far the intervention can influence it, and note any "supporting actions needed beyond core activities" (2015, pp. 130 to 132).

      In his nutrition example the analysis exposes a weak link. If husbands and mothers-in-law are unlikely to support mothers deciding what children eat, the programme will not work as designed. Some engagement with them is needed, perhaps through a partner more used to working on culture and gender (p. 132). The intervention becomes multifaceted, and the ToC grows a second pathway for that engagement.

      For an evaluator the same exercise does two jobs. It shows where the ToC is thin before you rely on it, and it tells you where evidence will matter most. Links resting on assumptions the programme does not control are the ones a contribution claim is most likely to break on, so they deserve the closest look. Analysing the ToC early, against prior research, can uncover weaknesses in the initial ideas before the evaluation is committed to them (p. 138).

      07Only nested theories of change carry the assumptions CA tests

      There is no single right drawing of a ToC. Mayne uses three, for different jobs (2019, p. 178):

      LevelWhat it showsUse
      Narrative ToCA brief account of how the intervention is meant to workCommunication
      Overview ToCThe pathways to impact, some steps on each, and the rationale, usually without causal link assumptionsOrientation
      Nested ToCsOne per pathway, place or actor group, with explicit causal link assumptionsThe level CA tests

      Where there are many pathways, a simplified ToC (activities, behaviour change, direct benefits, impact) can cut the data burden. Build the detailed nested version first, so you know what the simplified one is suppressing (pp. 180 to 181).

      The 2026 quality guidance describes a ToC fit for CA as having two linked parts (Ton et al. 2026, pp. 4 to 5). The first explains how the change of interest is expected to come about at all: its main causal factors and contextual conditions, drawn from formal theory, earlier research and evaluation, and stakeholders' experience. The second explains how and where the intervention influences that process, and each such explanation is a potential contribution claim. In simpler cases both parts fit one pathway. In complex ones, the first part may be a system map, the ToC may carry several pathways, each a separate claim, and nested ToCs let the team zoom in and out (p. 5). Hence, in this course's reading, the order matters: understand how the change happens first, then place the intervention in it, before tracing the programme's own pathway from its activities.

      08Test the public theory, not only the diagram

      Mayne suggests keeping at least three versions of a ToC (2015, p. 135). The first is a narrative of a sentence or two, the story managers or politicians tell when asked why the intervention will work. His example is an anti-smoking television advertisement: by describing the dangers of smoking, smokers will stop. For the nutrition programme, the narrative is that educating mothers about nutritious diets will lead them to improve their children's diets (p. 135).

      Each narrative rests on what Mayne calls a rationale assumption. Here it is that better information will change behaviour (p. 135). Because the narrative is how the intervention is publicly described and defended, he calls it "the public theory of change" (p. 135). An overview version then shows the big picture of a multifaceted intervention, with its nested ToCs and rationale assumptions, and a detailed version carries the causal link assumptions that CA tests (pp. 135 to 136).

      The nuance is that the rationale assumption is often the weakest part of the whole theory, and the one least likely to be written down. COM-B treats capability, opportunity and motivation as all needed for behaviour change (Mayne 2019, p. 179). Information on its own mostly adds capability. Hence, it is worth stating the rationale assumption explicitly and asking early whether the evidence supports it. If it fails, no amount of evidence on the detailed links will rescue the claim.

      09Build it with people, and expect it to change

      Mayne suggests building a ToC in a participatory way where possible. A group can start from a blank page, or, often more efficiently, from a "straw" version drafted by a few people and then opened to comment and challenge (2015, p. 138). Discussion may surface more than one version of how the intervention works. Rather than forcing agreement, it can be useful to test both against what happened (p. 138).

      Stakeholder views are not enough on their own. The ToC should also draw on prior research (p. 138), which is why the COM-B model above is useful: it brings research on behaviour into the room. The aim is a "good enough" ToC rather than a perfect one (p. 138).

      Finally, a ToC is a hypothesis, not a fixed plan. Mayne describes ToCs as "models of how change is expected to happen" before an intervention, or of how it has happened afterwards (p. 121). In complex settings he expects initial ToCs to be revised as new knowledge comes in (p. 135). Hence, an evaluator should record which version of the ToC a claim was tested against, and why it changed. A ToC revised quietly to fit the evidence is a weaker basis for a claim than one revised openly.

      Seven questions that ask you to use the ideas on cases you have not seen. Each answer explains itself, and you can retry the ones you miss.

      PracticeWrite the missing assumptions

      Case, invented for practice A school WASH programme in Arua builds handwashing stations next to latrines and trains a school health club. Its ToC has one arrow: "Handwashing stations built, so pupils wash hands with soap after using the latrine." Write the causal link assumptions this arrow needs. Use COM-B to check you have covered all three elements.

      Capability. Pupils know when and how to wash hands with soap, taught by the health club.
      Opportunity. Water and soap are at the stations every school day; stations are unlocked, close to the latrines and usable by small children; there is time between lessons.
      Motivation. Teachers and club members prompt and model handwashing, and it becomes what pupils expect of each other.
