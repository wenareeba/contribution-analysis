    05 / 08Contribution Analysis

    Size and relative importance

    Funders want to know how much of a change their money bought. This lesson is about answering them honestly: what CA cannot say, and the defensible things it can. Nine short sections, one exercise to try, seven questions and one reply to draft.

    On this page
      By the end of this lesson

      You can explain to a commissioner what CA can and cannot say about how big a contribution was, and offer a defensible way to judge relative importance.

      01CA cannot put a percentage on a contribution

      Ton and colleagues take the funder's question seriously and set out the tension between what commissioners ask for and what the method can deliver (Ton et al. 2019, p. 7).

      Mayne's position is clear. Packages of necessary factors bring about change, not any single factor, so "CA does not, on its own, estimate the size or indeed the relative importance of the causal factors at work" (2019, p. 181). Among CA's limits he lists that it "cannot determine how much of an outcome result can be attributed to an intervention" (p. 187).Every factor in a causal package is necessary, so in that sense all are equal (Mayne 2015, pp. 128 to 129). That is why "40% due to the programme" has no meaning inside a single causal package.

      02Mayne's view of "how much" changed over twenty years

      It helps to know that Mayne did not always hold this position. Markus Palenberg, who worked with him for over a decade, traces the change. Mayne's first papers on the method were titled "Addressing Attribution Through Contribution Analysis", and in 1999 and 2001 he wrote that CA might give "a reasonable estimate of the magnitude of the impact" (Palenberg 2023, p. 397). From about 2012, as he brought INUS and probabilistic ideas into CA, he separated it more clearly from the "how much" question and from counterfactual causality, and largely stopped using attribution language (p. 397).

      Yet the question stayed on his mind. Palenberg reports that Mayne felt CA had to offer more than a yes or no on whether an intervention played a causal role, which may explain his later guidance on ranking the importance of causal factors and his part in work on rankings and effect sizes (p. 398). Palenberg also notes that CA can take in counterfactual findings where they exist, while remaining open to other ways of showing importance (p. 399).

      Hence, older CA texts that talk about attribution are not simply wrong, and recent ones that refuse any talk of size are not the whole story. The settled point is narrower: CA on its own does not produce a share.

      03Relative importance depends on how you define importance

      Mayne says relative importance can still be explored, once you decide what "importance" means. He lists four perspectives (2019, p. 181), which Palenberg also discusses (2023, pp. 394 to 395). Each gives a different answer, so the evaluation must say which it used.

      Case, invented for practice. Stunting fell in a district. Three things contributed: a donor-funded counselling programme for mothers, government health workers who delivered it, and micronutrient powders from a second donor. Switch the lens and watch the ranking move.

      Lens on importance

      How important do informed actors judge each factor to have been?

      1. Counselling for mothersRated highest by mothers and district nutrition staff.
      2. Health workersRated highest by health facility staff; second overall.
      3. Micronutrient powdersValued, but seen as secondary to changed feeding.

      What did each factor do in the causal story?

      1. Health workersEvery other factor reached households through them.
      2. Counselling for mothersChanged what mothers knew and did at meals.
      3. Micronutrient powdersAdded nutrients once feeding had changed.

      How much was invested in each factor?

      1. Health workersSalaries over the whole period are the largest cost.
      2. Micronutrient powdersCommodities and distribution.
      3. Counselling for mothersTraining and materials, the smallest budget.

      How hard was the change each factor helped bring about?

      1. Counselling for mothersOvercame feeding beliefs and resistance from in-laws, the hardest barrier.
      2. Health workersOvercame distance to remote villages.
      3. Micronutrient powdersLittle to overcome once households were reached.

      The four lenses are Mayne's (2019, p. 181). The case and its rankings are invented for this course. The counselling programme comes first under two lenses and last under one, which is why the lens must be stated.

      04CA can be stretched towards size, carefully

      Ton and colleagues ask whether CA can say anything about size, and answer that it can say more than nothing. Their options:

      • A hierarchy. Comparing contributions needs some sort of hierarchy among them (p. 5). Even where a share cannot be computed, a ranking is still possible (p. 9).
      • Confidence. Bayesian updating of confidence in each causal step (p. 7).
      • Interlinked designs. Combining statistical, survey and case evidence, as in their Ethiopian enterprise programme example (p. 9).
      • Detailed theories of change and system maps. Showing where the intervention sits among other contributors (p. 9).

      Their advice is to report plausible ranges rather than point estimates (p. 9).

      05Combined action and joint funding set hard limits

      Cautions from the same authors

      Where factors only work in combination, a size estimate for each is impossible (Ton et al. 2019, p. 9).

      Where interventions are interlinked, look at them together: both, or neither, can claim the full effect (p. 7).

      06Sometimes the actor matters less than the type of support

      Ton and colleagues work through the case of CDC, the UK's development finance institution, and they show why separating one funder's contribution can be harder than it looks. Evidence of CDC's role might come from firms or investors who mention it. Often, however, there is no such mention, because CDC is invisible to the firms it affects, for example when it invests through consortia or banks (2019, pp. 6 to 7).

      Linked interventions raise a second difficulty. Where another DFID programme improves the investment climate and CDC encourages fund managers to use the new opportunities, they suggest treating the two together rather than evaluating each one separately (p. 7). And where the aim is to learn about a way of working, they suggest the more useful analysis is of the kind of intervention, kept separate from who delivers it (p. 7).

      For a funder, this is a useful reframing. "What did our money do?" may have no clean answer. "Does this kind of support, delivered alongside these partners, contribute to this change, and how?" often does, and it is the more useful question for deciding what to fund next.

      07The funder's question is legitimate, even when it cannot be answered

      Ton and colleagues do not dismiss the question. Commissioners often need some sense of the size of the contribution they funded, not least to report at an aggregate level to parliament. They call the result a paradox: "legitimate but unanswerable" questions that evaluations must somehow reconcile with what is possible (2019, p. 7).

      Aggregation sharpens the problem. When two interventions depend on each other, both can claim the whole result, or neither can claim any of it. Either way, adding results across a portfolio counts the same change more than once, which matters for donors, for benchmarking schemes and for any bonus tied to results (p. 7). The intuitive answer is that each claims a part, since they contributed together. But how much each should claim has no single objective answer (p. 7).

      For a portfolio manager this is the real lesson of the lesson. Contribution claims from separate evaluations of linked interventions cannot simply be summed.

      The 2026 quality guidance offers a vocabulary for this conversation. Each contribution claim should carry an assessment of its significance relative to the other factors and conditions at play, and of its stability, meaning how likely it is to change with further data collection (Ton et al. 2026, pp. 6 to 7). Neither is a percentage. However, together they tell a funder how significant the intervention's role was relative to other factors, and how firm that judgement is.

      08How one evaluation built a range instead of a point

      Ton and colleagues describe an evaluation of the Private Enterprise Programme Ethiopia (PEPE) that produced a plausible range for additional jobs, with lower and upper bounds rather than one figure (2019, pp. 8 to 9). It linked four research components, each checking a different step of the ToC.

      ComponentWhat it contributed to the estimate
      Monitoring dataWhat each part of the programme delivered, and which change processes looked most significant
      Case studiesWhether service providers really improved because of the programme, checked critically against reporting, literature and interviews; effects showed up in only some sectors
      Firm surveyA regression comparing supported and unsupported firms, giving a confidence interval for the change in profits; a second estimate from firms' own assessments of the programme's influence
      Economy-wide modelLow and high scenarios applied only in sectors where the case studies found a clear contribution, giving the range of jobs

      Paraphrased from Ton et al. (2019, pp. 8 to 9).

      Two features are worth copying. Each component informed the next, so the case studies limited where the model was allowed to assume effects. And the authors were open about a weakness: the programme may have chosen firms that were already doing better, and they controlled only for factors likely to be linked to that choice (p. 8). A range built this way is honest about its own uncertainty, which is its strength. It also took a substantial mix of research, which Ton and colleagues say such estimates require (p. 9).

      09What a defensible answer to the funder contains

      Putting the lesson together, a reply to "how much of this was us?" can say several true things without inventing a share. It can state the claim: the intervention contributed, and how. It can name its role, for example that it triggered the change or strengthened one already under way. It can rank it against other contributors under a stated definition of importance, which Ton and colleagues consider possible once the ToC is refined enough to acknowledge other contributors (2019, p. 9). And where resources allow linked quantitative work, it can give a plausible range (p. 9).

      What it should not do is turn a ranking into a percentage, or present one actor's range as if partners played no part. Where an intervention depended entirely on others, Ton and colleagues are blunt: attribution to one of them is impossible, "but not necessarily needed" (p. 9).

      PracticeAnswer the funder

      The request "We funded the district nutrition programme alongside two other donors and the government. Tell us what percentage of the fall in stunting is due to our money." Draft a short, honest reply that explains what CA can offer instead, and proposes one way to judge relative importance.

      A Contribution Analysis can show whether your funding was a necessary part of the package that reduced stunting, and how it worked. It cannot give a defensible percentage, because the fall came from several factors that only worked together (Mayne 2019, pp. 181, 187; Ton et al. 2019, p. 9). What we can offer is a judgement of relative importance on a lens you choose. For example, we can set out the role your funding played: which barriers it removed that others did not, and how hard those barriers were to overcome. We will present this as a ranking with a stated level of confidence, not a single figure.
