Size and relative importance

1. The question commissioners keep asking

Funders often want to know how much of a change their money bought. Ton and colleagues take that demand seriously and set out the tension between what commissioners ask for and what the method can deliver (Ton et al. 2019, p. 7).

Mayne's position is clear. Because packages of necessary factors bring about change, not any single factor, CA does not on its own estimate the size or relative importance of the causal factors at work (2019, p. 181). Among CA's limits he lists that it cannot determine how much of an outcome can be attributed to an intervention (p. 187).

Every factor in a causal package is necessary, so in that sense all are equal (Mayne 2015, pp. 128 to 129). That is why "40% due to the programme" has no meaning inside a single causal package.

2. Relative importance is possible, if you define it first

Mayne says relative importance can still be explored, once you decide what "importance" means (2019, p. 181). He lists four perspectives, which Palenberg (2023, pp. 394 to 395) also discusses:

LensThe question it answersExample evidence
Perceived importanceHow important do informed actors judge each factor to have been?Structured ratings by people with different vantage points
Role playedWhat did each factor do in the causal story?The causal narrative: which barrier each factor removed
Funds expendedHow much was invested in each factor?Budget and expenditure records
Extent of the constraints to changeHow hard was the change each factor helped bring about?Evidence on the constraints each factor overcame

Each lens gives a different answer, so say which one you used and why.

3. Stretching CA towards size

Ton et al. (2019) ask whether CA can say anything about size, and answer that it can say more than nothing. Their options:

  • A hierarchy. Comparing contributions needs some sort of hierarchy among them (p. 5). Even where a share cannot be computed, a ranking is still possible (p. 9).
  • Confidence. Bayesian updating of confidence in each causal step (p. 8).
  • Interlinked designs. Combining statistical, survey and case evidence, as in their Ethiopian enterprise programme example (p. 9).
  • Detailed ToCs and system maps. Showing where the intervention sits among other contributors (p. 10).

Their advice is to report plausible ranges rather than point estimates (p. 10).

Cautions from the same authors

Where factors only work in combination, a size estimate for each is impossible (p. 9). And where interventions are interlinked, look at them together: both, or neither, can claim the full effect (p. 7).

Practice: answer the commissioner

A donor writes: "We funded the district nutrition programme alongside two other donors and the government. Tell us what percentage of the fall in stunting is due to our money." Draft a short, honest reply that explains what CA can offer instead, and proposes one way to judge relative importance.

A contribution analysis can show whether your funding was a necessary part of the package that reduced stunting, and how it worked. It cannot give a defensible percentage, because the fall came from several factors that only worked together (Mayne 2019, pp. 181, 187; Ton et al. 2019, p. 9). What we can offer is a judgement of relative importance on a lens you choose. For example, we can set out the role your funding played: which barriers it removed that others did not, and how hard those barriers would have been to overcome without it. We will present this as a ranking with a stated level of confidence, not a single figure.
