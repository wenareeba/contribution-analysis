1. Four ways of thinking about causation

Palenberg (2023) sets out four types of causality relevant to CA (pp. 390 to 391) and traces how Mayne drew on them:

AccountCore ideaIn CA
CounterfactualCompare what happened with what would have happened without the interventionMayne's thinking changed on whether CA could and should address counterfactual questions; from about 2012 he positioned it firmly as a method for generative causality (p. 397)
GenerativeCausation as a chain of cause and effect eventsEmbodied by the theories of change used in CA (p. 391)
INUSA cause is an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition; Mackie, 1965 (p. 391)Borrowed by Mayne, with causal packages, necessity and sufficiency (p. 395); he equated making a difference with being a contributory cause (p. 396)
ProbabilisticCauses raise the likelihood of an effectProbabilistic elements were integrated into the approach (abstract)

His conclusion is that CA can flexibly integrate elements from different causality types (abstract). He closes with Patton: "Rigorous thinking supersedes rigorous methods" (p. 399).

2. CA and process tracing

Befani and Mayne (2014) propose combining the two as a single approach to generative causal inference. CA supplies the theory of change and the contribution story; process tracing supplies tests of how strongly each piece of evidence supports a causal step. The result shifts the focus from assessing impact to assessing confidence about impact.

In Mayne's two-part claim, evidence on necessity supports the first part (the intervention contributed); the second part (how it contributed) needs the sufficiency argument and causal narrative (2019, p. 182). Process tracing tests sharpen the evidence; they do not replace the narrative.

The tests themselves are in Lesson 3: straw-in-the-wind, hoop, smoking gun and doubly decisive (Collier 2011, p. 825).

3. A family of theory-driven approaches

Leeuw (2023) compares CA with realist evaluation (RE) and the policy-scientific approach (PSA):

Contribution analysisRealist evaluationPolicy-scientific approach
Core questionDid the intervention contribute, and how?What works, for whom, in what circumstances?Does the theory behind the policy hold?
MechanismsMainly "process mechanisms" (p. 409)Leeuw contrasts CA's process focus with the behavioural mechanisms the other approaches look for (p. 416)
Stance towards the theoryMainly constructive, a "helpdesk" for policy makers (p. 415)Weeds out alternative theories of how a programme works (Pawson and Tilley 2004, p. 17)Sets out to test, challenge and criticise the theory, not to validate it (p. 415)

Leeuw also sets out the ground the three share (p. 414) and concludes that they belong to one family of theory-driven evaluation (pp. 417 to 418). He finds CA less precise and testable than the other two (p. 414), and notes it pays no explicit attention to learning from evaluations of similar interventions (p. 416).

4. Choosing for the question

If the question is mainly...Lead with
Did this intervention contribute, and how?CA, with process tracing tests for key links
For whom and in which contexts does it work?Realist evaluation, or CA with nested ToCs by group and place
Are the policy's assumptions sound?Policy-scientific approach
How much difference, compared with no intervention?A counterfactual design, with CA to explain how

Practice: choose and combine

A commissioner wants to know whether a national school feeding policy improved attendance, for which groups of children, and whether the policy's assumptions about household food security hold. Propose a design that combines approaches, and say what each part answers.

Contribution. A CA with nested ToCs by region, testing whether feeding contributed to attendance and how, with process tracing tests on the key link from meals to attendance.
For whom. Realist-informed analysis of context, mechanism and outcome for groups such as girls, younger children and children in food-insecure districts.
Assumptions. A policy-scientific reconstruction of the policy's assumptions about household food security, restated as testable propositions and checked against survey and market data.
