    CA among theory-based approaches

    CA is one of several ways to reason about cause from a theory of the programme. Knowing its neighbours tells you when to lead with CA, when to combine it, and when another approach fits the question better. Nine short sections, one design tool to try, seven questions and one design to propose.

      01CA borrows from four ways of thinking about cause

      Palenberg sets out four types of causality relevant to CA (2023, pp. 390 to 391) and traces how Mayne drew on them.

      TypeCore ideaIn CA
      CounterfactualCompare what happened with what would have happened without the interventionMayne's thinking changed on whether CA could and should address counterfactual questions; from about 2012 he "positioned it firmly as a method for assessing generative causality" (p. 397)
      GenerativeCausation as a series of linked cause and effect stepsThis is the view the theories of change in CA express (p. 391)
      INUSA cause is an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition; Mackie, 1965 (p. 391)Borrowed by Mayne with causal packages, necessity and sufficiency (p. 395); he equated making a difference with being a contributory cause (p. 396)
      ProbabilisticCauses raise the likelihood of an effectProbabilistic elements were integrated into the approach (abstract)

      Palenberg concludes that CA can draw on more than one type of causality and combine them (abstract), and closes with Patton: "Rigorous thinking supersedes rigorous methods" (p. 399).

      02Process tracing sharpens CA's evidence but does not replace its narrative

      Befani and Mayne propose combining the two as a single approach to generative causal inference (2014). CA supplies the theory of change and the contribution story; process tracing supplies tests of how strongly each piece of evidence supports a causal step. The result shifts the focus from "assessing impact" to "assessing confidence" about impact (2014).

      In Mayne's two-part claim, evidence on necessity supports the first part, that the intervention contributed; the second part, how it contributed, needs the sufficiency argument and causal narrative (2019, p. 182).The four tests themselves, straw-in-the-wind, hoop, smoking gun and doubly decisive, are in Lesson 3 (Collier 2011, p. 825). The tests sharpen the evidence; they do not replace the narrative.

      03A design review that put contributory causes at the centre

      In 2012 a study for the UK Department for International Development (DFID) reviewed designs and methods for impact evaluation. Its conclusion was that most development interventions are "contributory causes": they work as part of a causal package alongside other helping factors, among them how stakeholders behave, related programmes, institutional capacity and wider trends (Stern et al. 2012, p. ii). It followed that "Did the intervention make a difference?" is often a more informative question than "Did the intervention work?", because it leaves room for combinations of causes (p. ii).

      The study then matched four key evaluation questions to designs (pp. 37 and 48).

      Key questionAssumesDesigns it suggests
      How much of a measured net impact is due to the intervention?Outcomes and intervention can be clearly specified; the intervention can be manipulatedExperiments; statistical studies; hybrids
      Has the intervention made a difference?Several relevant causes; the intervention is one part of a causal packageExperiments; theory-based evaluation such as CA; case-based designs such as QCA
      How has the intervention made a difference?Interventions interact with other causal factorsTheory-based evaluation, especially realist variants; participatory approaches
      Can this be expected to work elsewhere?What worked in one place can work in anotherParticipatory approaches; natural experiments; synthesis studies

      Paraphrased and shortened from Stern et al. (2012, Table 4.2, p. 48).

      One warning from the same study deserves a place in every design. There is a trade-off between a programme's scope and the strength of causal inference. Claims are easier for narrowly defined interventions, so it is tempting to split large programmes into parts. Doing so risks missing the synergies between parts and judging success or failure on an incomplete analysis (p. ii).

      04Each logic of inference needs different things, and has its own blind spot

      The same study compared four broad approaches to causal inference by what they require and where they are weak (Stern et al. 2012, Table 3.1, p. 18).

      ApproachRequiresStrengthWeakness
      RegularityMany, or highly diverse, casesUncovering general patternsExplaining how and why
      Experiments and counterfactualsTwo identical cases for comparison; control over the interventionAvoiding biasGeneralisation; the role of context
      Multiple causationEnough comparable casesDiscovering typologiesInterpreting highly complex combinations
      Generative and mechanismsGood access to multiple data sources; theory to identify supporting factorsIn-depth understanding; fine-grained explanation; the role of contextEstimating extent; risks of bias and loss of independence

      Paraphrased from Stern et al. (2012, p. 18).

      CA sits in the last row, and the row is a fair summary of the earlier lessons. Its strengths are explanation and context. Its blind spots are size, which Lesson 5 covers, and bias, which is why Lessons 3 and 7 spend so long on challenge. The study's answer to these trade-offs is to match designs to settings and to combine designs so that one compensates for another (p. 18).

      05CA, realist evaluation and the policy-scientific approach are one family

      Leeuw compares CA with realist evaluation and the policy-scientific approach (2023).

      Contribution AnalysisRealist evaluationPolicy-scientific approach
      Core questionDid the intervention contribute, and how?What works, for whom, in what circumstances?Does the theory behind the policy hold?
      MechanismsMainly "process mechanisms" (p. 409)Leeuw contrasts CA's process focus with the behavioural mechanisms the other approaches look for (p. 416)
      Stance towards the theoryMainly constructive, a "helpdesk" for policy makers (p. 415)Weeds out alternative theories of how a programme works (Pawson and Tilley 2004, p. 17)Sets out to test, challenge and criticise the theory, not to validate it (p. 415)

      Leeuw also sets out the ground the three share (p. 414) and concludes that they belong to one family of theory-driven evaluation (pp. 417 to 418). He finds CA less precise and testable than the other two (p. 414), and notes that it pays no explicit attention to learning from evaluations of similar interventions (p. 416).

      06Realist evaluation in its own terms

      Realist evaluation is CA's closest neighbour, so it is worth reading on its own terms. Pawson and Tilley put its question this way: not "What works?" but "What works for whom in what circumstances and in what respects, and how?" (2004, p. 2). Programmes, in their account, are theories that are embedded in social systems and that depend on people acting on them (pp. 3 to 5).

      Their example of the last point is dental health. Fluoridated water works whenever it is swallowed, with no effort from anyone. A campaign urging people to brush twice a day works only if people take up the message, and they may ignore, forget or reject it (p. 5). Hence their central idea, that "it is not programmes that work but the resources they offer to enable their subjects to make them work" (p. 6). The way people interpret and act on those resources is the mechanism, and the evaluation develops and tests context, mechanism and outcome configurations that explain why outcomes vary (p. 9).

      The difference from CA is one of emphasis rather than kind. CA asks whether a package that includes the intervention brought about a result, and how; realist evaluation expects the answer to vary by context and builds the evaluation around explaining that variation. A CA that takes "for whom" seriously, as Lesson 7's equity critique urges, moves towards realist questions.

      08Three common ways CA is combined with other designs

      Stern and colleagues recommend combining designs so that one compensates for another's weakness (2012, p. 18). Three combinations recur in the sources this course has used.

      • CA with process tracing. The combination set out earlier in this lesson, where CA frames the claim and process tracing weighs the evidence on each step.
      • CA with a quantitative estimate. Where a counterfactual finding exists, CA can incorporate it, and it can also use other ways of showing relative importance (Palenberg 2023, p. 399). The PEPE evaluation in Lesson 5 went further, linking survey regressions and an economy-wide model to case studies (Ton et al. 2019, pp. 8 to 9).
      • CA with participatory and case-based work. Embedded case studies can follow a ToC link by link (Delahais and Toulemonde 2012, p. 285), comparing cases is a common way in CA to check causal link assumptions (Ton et al. 2026, p. 8), and participatory approaches bring in the people whose behaviour the ToC depends on.

      Combining designs is not free. Each part has its own assumptions, and the report has to say which part supports which link of the claim. Otherwise the combination adds methods without adding strength.

      09From this introduction to mastery

      This course is an introduction for people new to Contribution Analysis. It gives you the vocabulary, the logic and the main debates, but it does not make you an expert. Mastery comes from three things that no course can replace.

      • Read the sources in depth. Start with Mayne (2019), then his 2015 article on theories of change, then the critiques and the applications in the bibliography. Each lesson's "Read next" box gives the order.
      • Apply the method, at growing scale. Begin with a single project, where the theory of change is manageable and you can see every link. Move to a regional programme with several pathways, then to a portfolio of linked interventions, where Lesson 5's warnings about adding up contributions apply. In time, an international programme brings nested theories of change across countries and partners.
      • Have your work challenged. Use the red team protocol in this repository on your own designs and reports, and ask a colleague who did not do the work to read the contribution story.

      A good next read, once you have run or closely reviewed one CA, is the 2026 quality guidance by Ton, Delahais, Koleros and Apgar. Its authors are explicit that it is "not intended to serve as an introduction to CA", but to support experienced evaluators and to inform commissioners (Ton et al. 2026, p. 1). That is the step this course prepares you for.

      Each step up in scale tests something new: more pathways, more partners, more contested evidence and more people with a stake in the answer. That is where the judgement the method depends on is built.

      PracticePropose a combined design

      Case, invented for practice A commissioner wants to know whether a national school feeding policy improved attendance, for which groups of children, and whether the policy's assumptions about household food security hold. Propose a design that combines approaches, and say what each part answers.

      Contribution. A CA with nested theories of change by region, testing whether feeding contributed to attendance and how, with process tracing tests on the key link from meals to attendance.
      For whom. Realist-informed analysis of context, mechanism and outcome for groups such as girls, younger children and children in food-insecure districts.
      Assumptions. A policy-scientific reconstruction of the policy's assumptions about household food security, restated as testable propositions and checked against survey and market data.
