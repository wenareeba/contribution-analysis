1. Two parts of the claim need two kinds of evidence

A contribution claim says the intervention contributed, and how. Mayne (2019, p. 182) ties each part to a different job for the evidence:

  • Necessity shows the intervention was an essential member of the causal package. That supports part 1: it contributed.
  • Sufficiency shows the package as a whole was enough to bring about the change. That supports part 2: here is how.

Mayne adds a warning about sufficiency. Data showing that each result and assumption happened is not enough on its own. You also need a credible causal narrative that explains why the package worked (pp. 182 to 183).

Mayne draws on Pearl and Mackenzie's point that statistics alone cannot establish causation without good explanatory theory (2019, pp. 182 to 183).

2. Six tools, and the question each one answers

Mayne's Table 1 (2019, p. 184) groups the evidence tools by what they can do. Read the right-hand column as the question to put to your evidence.

What hoops can and cannot do

Failing a hoop test makes causality very unlikely. Passing one does not confirm it, because other causal factors may also be at work (Mayne 2019, p. 183). A pile of passed hoops is still a pile of hoops.

3. The four process tracing tests

Mayne borrows two test types from process tracing. Collier (2011, Table 1, p. 825) sets out the full set of four, classified by whether passing the test is necessary and whether it is sufficient for affirming a causal inference:

TestNecessary?Sufficient?If passedIf failed
Straw-in-the-windNoNoAffirms relevance; does not confirmHypothesis weakened slightly, not eliminated
HoopYesNoAffirms relevance; does not confirmHypothesis eliminated
Smoking gunNoYesHypothesis confirmedWeakened somewhat, not eliminated
Doubly decisiveYesYesConfirmed, and rivals eliminatedHypothesis eliminated

Collier calls these categories a useful heuristic that should not be applied rigidly (p. 825). In Mayne's table, the time sequence, effort and expected-effects checks work as hoops, and unique effects work as a smoking gun (2019, pp. 183, 186). Doubly decisive evidence is rare in real evaluations.

4. From proof to confidence

Befani and Mayne (2014) combined CA with process tracing. They argue that process tracing tests and Bayesian principles can judge the strength of qualitative and mixed evidence inside a CA, shifting impact evaluation from "assessing impact" to "assessing confidence" about impact.

The practical idea is simple. For each piece of evidence, ask two questions. How likely would I be to find this if the claim were true? And how likely if it were false? Evidence you would expect to see either way tells you little. Evidence that is likely only if the claim is true moves your confidence a long way.

Ton and colleagues describe updating confidence in each causal step as evidence arrives (Ton et al. 2019, p. 8).

For a worked introduction, see Punton and Barnett's (2018) Itad brief on CA and Bayesian confidence updating, listed in the bibliography.

Case (invented). A climate adaptation programme in Kotido trains farmers to plant drought-tolerant sorghum. Yields rose in villages where farmers adopted the variety. For the link "farmers trained → farmers plant the new variety", list one piece of evidence for each of: verification, a hoop test, the sufficiency narrative, and a smoking gun. Say what each would show.

Verification. Seed distribution records and farm visits show trained farmers planted the variety.
Hoop (time sequence). Planting followed the training season, not before it.
Sufficiency narrative. Farmers explain that training showed them spacing and planting dates, and that seed was available locally on credit, which overcame the cost barrier.
Smoking gun. Farmers use a planting technique taught only in the programme's demonstration plots.
