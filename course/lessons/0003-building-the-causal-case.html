    Contribution analysis, lesson 3 of 8

    Building the causal case

    Each piece of evidence can do one job in a contribution claim, and only one. This lesson teaches you to tell which job, and to know what a pile of evidence can and cannot prove. Five short sections, two exercises to try, five questions and one evidence plan to write.

    On this page
      By the end of this lesson

      You can sort any piece of evidence by what it can and cannot show about a causal link, and assemble the evidence for both parts of a contribution claim.

      1The two parts of the claim need two kinds of evidence

      A contribution claim says the intervention contributed, and how. Mayne ties each part to a different job for the evidence (2019, p. 182):

      • Necessity shows the intervention was an essential member of the causal package. That supports part 1: it contributed.
      • Sufficiency shows the package as a whole was enough to bring about the change. That supports part 2: here is how.

      Mayne adds a warning about sufficiency. Data showing that each result and assumption happened is not enough on its own. You also need a credible causal narrative that explains why the package worked (pp. 182 to 183).Mayne draws on Pearl and Mackenzie's point that statistics alone cannot establish causation without good explanatory theory (2019, pp. 182 to 183).

      2Each of Mayne's six tools answers a different question

      Mayne's Table 1 groups the evidence tools by what they can do (2019, p. 184). Read the last column as the question to put to your evidence.

      ToolKindAsk of your evidence
      Verify the pathway and assumptionsChecking that change occurredDid each result and each causal link assumption actually happen? Without this, no claim about that link is possible (p. 183).
      Logical, plausible time sequenceHoopDid causes come before effects, and assumptions before the results they support? A result appearing earlier than expected may point to something else at work (pp. 183 to 184).
      Reasonable effort expendedHoopWas the intervention, with its partners, big enough to produce results of this size? (pp. 184 to 185)
      Expected effects realisedHoopIf the link worked, what secondary effects should appear? Are they there? (p. 185)
      Causal packages are sufficientBuilding the narrativeWhat barriers did the assumptions overcome, and what mechanisms were at work? (p. 185)
      Some unique effects observedConfirming a causal factorIs there an effect that could only appear if the intervention was part of the package? (p. 186)
      What hoops can and cannot do

      Failing a hoop makes causality very unlikely. Passing one does not confirm it, because other causal factors may also be at work (Mayne 2019, p. 183). A pile of passed hoops is still a pile of hoops.

      3Sort the evidence before you weigh it

      Case, invented for practice. A district programme in Kasese trains village health teams to counsel mothers on child feeding, using a picture card designed only for this programme. Sort each piece of evidence by the job it does.

      Choose the tool each finding belongs to

      Session registers show 90% of planned sessions were held.

      Diets improved after the sessions began, not before.

      Mothers can describe the advice several weeks later.

      Mothers explain that their in-laws agreed to new meals after a health worker visited the household.

      Households use the programme's own picture card at meal times.

      4Process tracing classifies tests by necessity and sufficiency

      Mayne borrows two test types from process tracing. Collier sets out all four, classified by whether passing is necessary and whether it is sufficient for affirming a causal inference (2011, Table 1, p. 825).

      TestNecessarySufficientIf passedIf failed
      Straw-in-the-windNoNoAffirms relevance; does not confirmWeakened slightly, not eliminated
      HoopYesNoAffirms relevance; does not confirmEliminated
      Smoking gunNoYesConfirmedWeakened somewhat, not eliminated
      Doubly decisiveYesYesConfirmed, and rivals eliminatedEliminated

      Collier calls these categories a useful heuristic that should not be applied rigidly (p. 825). In Mayne's table, the time sequence, effort and expected effects checks work as hoops, and unique effects work as a smoking gun (2019, pp. 183, 186). Doubly decisive evidence is rare in real evaluations.

      5Ask how likely the evidence is if the claim were false

      Befani and Mayne combined CA with process tracing. They argue that process tracing tests and Bayesian principles can judge the strength of qualitative and mixed evidence inside a CA, shifting the work from "assessing impact" to "assessing confidence" about impact (2014).Ton and colleagues describe updating confidence in each causal step as evidence arrives (2019, p. 8). Punton and Barnett's (2018) Itad brief is a short worked introduction; it is in the bibliography. The practical idea is two questions per finding. Try them.

      If the claim is true, how likely is this finding?

      If the claim is false, how likely is it?

      Evidence you would expect to see either way tells you little. Evidence that is likely only if the claim is true is what moves confidence.

      PracticePlan the evidence for one link

      Case, invented for practice A climate adaptation programme in Kotido trains farmers to plant drought-tolerant sorghum. Yields rose in villages where farmers adopted the variety. For the link "farmers trained, so farmers plant the new variety", name one piece of evidence for each of verification, a hoop test, the sufficiency narrative and a smoking gun, and say what each would show.

      Verification. Seed distribution records and farm visits show trained farmers planted the variety.
      Hoop, time sequence. Planting followed the training season, not before it.
      Sufficiency narrative. Farmers explain that training showed them spacing and planting dates, and that seed was available locally on credit, which overcame the cost barrier.
      Smoking gun. Farmers use a planting technique taught only on the programme's demonstration plots.
