    Contribution analysis, lesson 6 of 8

    Running a CA in practice

    The method is clear on paper. Running it on a real budget, with real deadlines and missing data, is where it succeeds or fails. Four short sections, one figure to explore, five questions and one scoping note to write.

      1The steps are a cycle, not a line

      BetterEvaluation presents Mayne's six steps as an iterative cycle, with a diagram from Apgar, Hernandez and Ton (2020). Step 6 feeds back into steps 4 and 2 (Apgar and Levine 2024). Mayne's 2019 version makes the return to step 4 an explicit seventh step (Box 1, p. 173).Wimbush, Montague and Mulherin (2012) added a different seventh step: using CA to support organisations or networks to review and improve their strategies (Apgar and Levine 2024). Select a step to see what it produces, and which template in this repository supports it.

      Contribution analysis as a cycle
      7. Step 6 feeds back to step 4, and to step 2 when the theory itself needs revising.

      Set out the attribution problem

      • The cause-effect question, word for word, and its type: contribution, causes of outcome, or relative importance.
      • A specific, time-bound result of interest.

      Supported by the CA design template, section 1.

      Develop the theory of change and its risks

      • Nested theories of change with explicit causal link assumptions.
      • The external factors that could act on each link.

      Supported by the ToC and assumptions worksheet and the other factors register.

      Gather the existing evidence

      • Monitoring data, documents and earlier studies, before any new fieldwork.
      • A first view of which links are well supported and which are not.

      Record it link by link in the ToC and assumptions worksheet.

      Assemble and assess the contribution story

      • A first contribution story, and the challenges to it.
      • The weakest links, which decide where new evidence goes.

      Check the draft claim against the contribution claim checklist.

      Seek out additional evidence

      • New data aimed at the weak links, not at everything.
      • For each link, what would count as passing and as failing, set before collection.

      Plan it in the evidence plan matrix.

      Revise and strengthen the story

      • A revised, two-part claim, with its limits stated.
      • A return to step 4, or to step 2 if the theory failed somewhere.

      Test the design and the draft against the red team protocol.

      Step names follow BetterEvaluation's presentation of Mayne's steps (Apgar and Levine 2024). What each step produces, and the template links, are this course's guidance.

      3Plan for more time than you think

      A scoping review of CA in health found seven studies (Buetti et al. 2025). The patterns are useful in any sector.

      • All seven said CA is time-consuming and resource-intensive. One took seven months and would have gone better with eight to ten.
      • Judgement calls were hard: what level of evidence is suitable, and which external factors to include.
      • Practical problems included stakeholders replaced after elections and slow access to government data. Six of the seven built their theory of change retrospectively.
      • Benefits: a flexible approach, actionable information for stakeholders, and a common framework and vocabulary.

      The authors suggest that standardised reporting guidelines or protocols could improve rigour, transparency and credibility.

      4Test the story's credibility before you publish it

      BetterEvaluation's worked example is Delahais and Toulemonde's (2017) evaluation of two forestry research centres' contribution to sustainable forest management in the Congo Basin. The theory of change had five contribution pathways. The team tested it with 65 stakeholder interviews, a review of 130 documents and three case studies.

      They then checked the credibility of the revised story three ways: the steering group reviewed all working documents, several researchers reviewed the first case study, and a reviewer examined the credibility of the key findings (Apgar and Levine 2024).

      PracticeWrite a scoping note

      Case, invented for practice You have eight weeks and two people to assess whether a district water programme contributed to fewer school days lost to diarrhoea. Write a five-line scoping note: the result of interest, the question type, which links new data collection will focus on, your evidence threshold for one key link, and how you will share emerging findings.

      Result. School days lost to diarrhoea among pupils in the 12 programme schools, 2024 to 2026.
      Question type. Contribution: did the programme contribute, and how?
      Focus links. Existing records cover construction; new data targets the weakest links, from stations built to pupils washing hands, and from handwashing to fewer absences.
      Threshold. For the handwashing link: spot checks in at least six schools on two unannounced days, plus pupil and teacher accounts that agree.
      Sharing. A short findings note to the district team at week five, before the draft report.
