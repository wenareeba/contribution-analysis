    01 / 08Contribution Analysis

    Read the question first

    The evaluation question decides how far you have to chase other factors. Get that right and every later judgement in a Contribution Analysis rests on firm ground. Eight short sections, a figure to try, seven questions and one claim to write.

    On this page
      By the end of this lesson

      You can write a contribution claim in Mayne's two-part form, and say for any evaluation question how much the evaluator has to examine other factors.

      Where this course sits

      This course introduces Contribution Analysis to people who are new to it. It is a starting point, not the end of the road. Mastery requires reading the sources in depth and applying the method on real evaluations, at the scale of a project, a region, a portfolio and, in time, an international programme. Each lesson ends with the readings to take next.

      01CA began as a way to report results honestly

      Contribution Analysis began in public management rather than in academic research. Mayne first published the approach in 1999 (Palenberg 2023, p. 389), and his 2001 article wrote for managers who were being asked to account for outcomes they did not fully control. He observed that performance reporting too often "completely ignores the attribution problem", either crediting the programme with every result or implying it by saying nothing about other factors (Mayne 2001, p. 7). His answer was a set of steps for building a credible "performance story" (p. 9), aimed at what Hendricks called "plausible association": whether "a reasonable person, knowing what has occurred in the program and that the intended outcomes actually occurred, agrees that the program contributed to those outcomes" (p. 8).

      The evidence was meant to come from several sources at once. No single piece is likely to convince, but a set of complementary evidence can; Mayne called this the "multiple lines of evidence" argument (p. 20). The 2008 brief kept the six steps of 2001 (Mayne 2001, p. 9), and called the end product a "contribution story" (Mayne 2008, p. 1). In 2019 Mayne revisited it: seven steps, opening with the specific cause-effect question and closing with a return to step 4 where needed (2019, p. 173).

      Hence, when you read CA guidance, check its date. The vocabulary moved from performance story to contribution story to contribution claim, and several positions changed with it. This course follows Mayne's 2019 statement unless it says otherwise.

      The method has kept moving since Mayne. In 2026 the World Bank's Independent Evaluation Group published quality guidance by Giel Ton, Thomas Delahais, Andrew Koleros and Marina Apgar. It organises CA around six steps based on Mayne (2019), grouped into two phases: theory building (steps 1 to 4) and theory testing (steps 5 and 6). Mayne's seventh step, a return to step 4, becomes the guidance's point that all the steps are applied iteratively and recursively rather than in sequence (Ton et al. 2026, pp. 1 to 3). The guidance was written partly because CA's popularity has produced a diversity of practice and some confusion about what CA entails (p. 1). Its six steps match Mayne's first six, so later lessons draw on the guidance where it adds to Mayne.

      02A contribution claim names one necessary part of a package

      Contribution Analysis (CA) takes a generative view of causality. Change comes about through a chain of cause and effect steps, and at each step a causal package of factors does the work together. The intervention is one member of that package.

      A contributory cause: "the intervention was one of several necessary or likely necessary factors in a causal package that together brought about or contributed to the changes observed".

      Mayne 2019, p. 174

      In Mackie's terms, each package member is an INUS condition (Insufficient but Necessary part of an Unnecessary but Sufficient condition). On its own it cannot produce the effect, the package fails without it, and the package itself is one of possibly several routes to the same effect (Mayne 2019, note 3)."Made a difference" in CA means the intervention played a positive causal role in the package. It does not mean "what would have happened without it", which is the counterfactual reading. Mayne draws that line on p. 174. Every member is necessary, so in a logical sense they are equally important. That is why CA does not say "the programme caused 40% of the change".

      Causal package. Tap a factor to switch it off.
      New feeding practicesProduced by this package

      Built on Mayne's own nutrition example (2019, pp. 177 and 186). The support factors are causal link assumptions in the theory of change.

      03Why "the programme caused it" is the wrong claim

      Mayne's 2012 brief on causal claims starts from a blunt point. Saying an intervention caused an impact is "much too simplistic" (Mayne 2012, ILAC Brief 26, p. 1). Most impacts, such as better health or reduced poverty, can be reached by several different interventions, so the intervention is rarely necessary. Many other factors are at work as well, so it is rarely sufficient either (p. 1). What remains is the contributory cause: an essential part of a package that, together, was enough.

      His worked example is a fish farming initiative for low-income farmers in northern Thailand (pp. 2 to 3). Laying it out shows how much of any result sits outside the programme's own activities.

      The initiative's componentsSupporting factors named in the report
      Participatory research on low-cost fish foodEnough farmers willing to try fish farming at the start
      Start-up funding for equipment and fingerlingsA market for fish beyond what families eat
      Advice on improved fish farming methodsAn affordable supply of healthy fingerlings; family support for the extra work; visible gains that neighbours could see; cash for feed and supplies

      Paraphrased from Mayne (2012, ILAC Brief 26, p. 2).

      The package is the components plus the supporting factors. It is not sufficient without them, and it is not necessary either, because household weaving businesses or better yields of existing crops could also raise incomes (p. 2). The initiative made a difference if two things hold: the results came about through the initiative and its supporting factors, and the initiative was an essential part of that package (p. 2).

      The example also shows what to do when results do not appear. Mayne's questions are whether a supporting factor failed, whether some other factor turned out to be needed, whether the activities were poorly delivered, and whether the causal model itself was sound (p. 3). A null result read this way still teaches you something about the theory.

      04The claim has two parts: that it contributed, and how

      Mayne says an evidence-based contribution claim always has two parts (2019, p. 182):

      1. The intervention contributed to the observed change. It played a positive role.
      2. It did so in the following manner: the causal narrative of how the package worked.

      Part 1 answers "Has the intervention (or component) made a difference?" Part 2 answers the question Mayne calls more interesting: "How and why has the intervention (or component) made a difference, or not, and for whom?" (pp. 174 to 175) Showing that the intervention was a necessary member gives you part 1. Showing that the package was sufficient, and how it worked, gives you part 2. A report that stops at part 1 has a verdict without an explanation.

      05The question decides how far you chase other factors

      This is the rule the rest of the course builds on. In 2011 Mayne suggested "a contribution claim could be made when external factors were shown not to have contributed significantly". In 2012 he called for exploring rival explanations. In 2019 he called both statements incorrect, because they ignored what multiple causal factors imply (p. 175).

      His corrected position: external factors are usually other causal factors at work, not rivals. You can test whether the intervention was a contributory cause, and how, without examining factors outside the package, unless they are causally linked to it. How much you examine them depends on the question (pp. 175 to 176).Mayne notes that Lemire, Nielsen and Dybdal (2012) concede in a footnote that alternative explanations are needed only to compare relative contribution (2019, p. 176).

      Question typeExampleOther factors
      ContributionDid the programme contribute to the outcome, and how?Need not play a major role, unless causally linked to the package
      Causes of outcomeWhat brought about the observed outcome?Must be explored as part of the answer
      Relative importanceWhich factors mattered most?Must be compared, with "importance" defined first

      The row labels are this course's terms, not Mayne's. Rows 1 and 2 follow his p. 176; row 3 extends his p. 181 discussion of relative importance.

      Check question fit

      Watch for a report that asks a contribution question and ends with a causes-of-outcome or ranking claim, such as "the programme was the main driver". The conclusion then answers a bigger question than the one set (Mayne 2019, p. 176).

      Before calling it overreach, check whether the design itself set out to answer the bigger question. The test is whether the claim matches the question the report set itself.

      06Most terms of reference need their question sharpened

      Step 1 is setting out the cause-effect question, and Mayne notes it is "often not adequately addressed" (2019, p. 176). The trouble is that some questions sound meaningful and are not. His example is "Has the intervention been effective?" Most interventions are not, on their own, the cause of what is observed, so the question has no clean answer. A usable contribution question names the result of interest and asks whether and how the intervention contributed to it (p. 176).

      As written in the terms of referenceAs a contribution question
      Has the programme been effective in reducing stunting?Did the programme contribute to the fall in stunting among children under five in the two districts, and if so, how and for whom?
      What is the impact of the weather information service?Did the service contribute to farmers changing planting dates, and through which pathway?
      Did the grant fund work?Did the grant fund contribute to firms adopting the new process, and what else had to be in place for it to do so?

      Course examples. Each rewrite names one result, a place or group, and asks both parts of the claim.

      Sharpening the question is also where you agree with commissioners what kind of answer they will get. If they need a ranking or a share, that is a different question, and Lesson 5 explains why CA alone cannot supply it.

      07CA fits some evaluations better than others

      Mayne is plain that CA is "not a quick-and-dirty approach" (2019, p. 187). He lists three costs and five strengths, and the balance between them is a useful first test of whether the method suits an evaluation.

      What it costsWhat it offers
      Often a substantial amount of data, and rigorous thinkingCausal inference where experimental and quasi-experimental designs are impossible or unnecessary
      A reasonably detailed and sound theory of changeAn account of why and how change happened, and for whom
      No estimate of how much of an outcome is due to the interventionA place in mixed-method designs, for example beside comparison groups that measure how much change occurred
      Inference about the intervention without necessarily examining external causal factors
      A way to handle settings with many causal factors at work

      Paraphrased from Mayne (2019, p. 187).

      The nuance sits in the third row. CA does not compete with designs that estimate size; it can sit beside them, each answering the question it is built for. Where a commissioner's main need is a number, CA on its own is the wrong tool. Where the need is to understand whether and how a programme mattered in a crowded field, it is often the right one.

      The 2026 guidance sharpens this first test in three ways. CA is most useful when the change in question extends beyond the intervention's direct influence, and so depends on several actors and factors whose mix varies by context (Ton et al. 2026, p. 3). Step 1 is also more than writing a question: the evaluator works with stakeholders to build a shared picture of the wider system and the intervention's place in it, which often leads to the question being reformulated and to a fresh look at whether CA still fits (p. 4). Finally, CA is mostly used after implementation, but its iterative logic can also support adaptive management, judging how plausible future changes are and how to reinforce them (p. 3).

      08Hoop tests keep a claim alive; a unique effect confirms a causal role

      Mayne's Table 1 lists the evidence tools (2019, p. 184). Four come from process tracing: three hoop tests and one smoking gun. Passing a hoop keeps the claim alive without confirming it (p. 183); a unique effect, the smoking gun, confirms that the intervention was a causal factor (p. 186).

      ToolWhat it showsStrength
      Verify the ToCPathway results and causal link assumptions happenedThe evidence base for any claim
      Plausible time sequenceCause came before effect, on a plausible timelineHoop: plausibility only
      Reasonable effortThe intervention was big enough to matterHoop: plausibility only
      Expected effects seenSecondary effects the link predicts appearedHoop: plausibility only
      Package is sufficientA causal narrative of how the package workedBuilds part 2 of the claim
      Unique effects seenAn effect only possible if the intervention was in the packageSmoking gun: confirms a causal factor

      Two limits to hold onto. Opinion about the contribution cannot ground the claim: interviews can show that results and assumptions happened, but a study resting only on opinion is not reporting on a CA (p. 175). And CA alone cannot estimate how much of an outcome is due to the intervention (pp. 181, 187).

      PracticeWrite the two-part claim

      Case, invented for practice A district programme in Kasese trained village health teams to counsel mothers on complementary feeding. Monitoring shows counselling sessions held on schedule, mothers able to describe the advice, and more households adding legumes to child meals within six months. Interviews show adoption was highest where the mother controlled the household food budget.

      Part 1. The counselling programme contributed to more households adding legumes to child meals in Kasese. Part 2. It did so by reaching mothers on schedule and building their knowledge of complementary feeding, which changed meals where the mother controlled the food budget; budget control was a necessary support factor in the package.
