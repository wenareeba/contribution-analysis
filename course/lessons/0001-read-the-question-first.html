Contribution analysis · Lesson 1 of 8

Read the question first

By the end of this page you can write a contribution claim in Mayne's two-part form and say, for any evaluation question, how far the evaluator has to chase other factors.

Why this lesson comes first

Every judgement about how a contribution analysis handles other factors rests on one rule from Mayne (2019): the question asked decides how much of the other factors you must examine. Apply that rule cold and the rest of the course builds on firm ground.

1. What a contribution claim says

Contribution analysis (CA) takes a generative view of causality. Change comes from a chain of cause-and-effect steps, and at each step a causal package of factors does the work together. The intervention is one member of that package.

An intervention is a contributory cause when it was one of several necessary or likely necessary factors in a causal package that together brought about the change. Mayne 2019, p. 174

In Mackie's terms each package member is an INUS condition: an insufficient but necessary part of a condition that is itself unnecessary but sufficient for the effect. Because every member is necessary, they are, in a logical sense, equally important. That is why CA does not say "the programme caused 40% of the change".

"Made a difference" in CA means the intervention played a positive causal role in the package. It does not mean "what would have happened without it", which is the counterfactual reading. Mayne draws that line explicitly on p. 174.

2. The claim has two parts

Mayne (2019, p. 182) says an evidence-based contribution claim always has two parts:

  1. The intervention contributed to the observed change. It played a positive role.
  2. It did so in the following manner: the causal narrative of how the package worked.

Part 1 answers "Has the intervention (or component) made a difference?" Part 2 answers the question Mayne calls more interesting: "How and why has the intervention (or component) made a difference, or not, and for whom?" pp. 174 to 175 A report that stops at part 1 has a verdict without an explanation. Showing that the package was sufficient gives you part 2; showing the intervention was a necessary member gives you part 1.

3. The question decides how far you chase other factors

This is the rule the rest of the course builds on. In 2011 Mayne wrote that a claim could be made when external factors were shown not to have contributed significantly. In 2012 he called for exploring rival explanations. In 2019 he called both statements incorrect, because they ignored what multiple causal factors imply. p. 175

His corrected position: external factors are usually just other causal factors at work, not rivals. You can test whether the intervention was a contributory cause, and how, without examining factors outside the package, unless they are causally linked to it. How much you examine them depends on the question. pp. 175 to 176

Mayne also notes that Lemire, Nielsen and Dybdal (2012) concede in a footnote that alternative explanations are needed only to compare relative contribution (p. 176). That footnote matters for your discussion section.

Check question fit

Watch for a report that asks a contribution question and ends with a causes-of-outcome or ranking claim ("the programme was the main driver"). The conclusion then answers a bigger question than the one set (Mayne 2019, p. 176).

Before calling it overreach, check whether the evaluation design itself set out to answer the bigger question. The test is whether the claim matches the question the report set itself.

4. The evidence that carries a claim

Mayne's Table 1 (2019, p. 184) lists the tools. Four of them come from process tracing: three hoop tests and one smoking gun.

Two limits to hold onto. Stakeholder opinion about the contribution cannot ground the claim. Interviews can supply evidence that results and assumptions happened, but a study built only on opinion about the contribution is not CA (p. 175). And CA alone cannot estimate how much of an outcome is due to the intervention (pp. 181 and 187).

Practice: write it

Case (invented for practice). A district programme in Kasese trained village health teams to counsel mothers on complementary feeding. Monitoring shows counselling sessions held on schedule, mothers able to describe the advice, and more households adding legumes to child meals within six months. Interviews show adoption was highest where the mother controlled the household food budget. Write the two-part contribution claim.

Part 1. The counselling programme contributed to more households adding legumes to child meals in Kasese. Part 2. It did so by reaching mothers on schedule and building their knowledge of complementary feeding, which led to changed meals where the mother controlled the food budget; budget control was a necessary support factor in the package.
