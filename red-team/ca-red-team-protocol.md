# CA red team protocol

A checklist for testing whether a Contribution Analysis followed the steps properly and whether its claims match its evidence. Use it on your own CA before you sign it off, on a report you are reviewing, or on a CA design before fieldwork.

It follows Mayne's seven steps (2019, Box 1, p. 173) and the conditions BetterEvaluation gives for a sufficiently developed contribution story (Apgar and Levine 2024). Page numbers refer to Mayne (2019) unless stated.

## How to use it

1. Work through the steps in order. For each check, record **Pass**, **Partial**, **Fail** or **Not applicable**, with the page or section of the report that shows it.
2. A **Fail** on any check marked **(critical)** means the report should not be called a CA as it stands, or its main claim must be narrowed.
3. Finish with the five verification questions at the end. Name at least one external check (a source document, a second reader, the original data) for each audit.
4. Be fair. Weak checks often reflect limits of time, money and access, not carelessness. The test is whether the claim matches the evidence, not whether the evaluator did everything possible.

## Step 1. The cause-effect question

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | **(critical)** The causal question is written down and the result of interest is specified | A named result at a named level of the ToC, not "was the programme effective?" | p. 176 |
| 1.2 | The question type is clear: contribution, causes-of-outcome, or relative importance | One type, or a stated combination | p. 176 |
| 1.3 | The required level of confidence is set against the decisions the evaluation will inform | Users and their decisions named | BetterEvaluation, step 1 |
| 1.4 | An initial plausibility check was made: given the intervention's size and the problem, is an important contribution likely? | Stated before data collection | BetterEvaluation, step 1; p. 185 |

## Step 2. A theory of change that can carry a claim

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | **(critical)** The ToC sets out causal link assumptions for each link, not just boxes and arrows | Assumptions written as events or conditions needed for the next result | pp. 172, 176 |
| 2.2 | Assumptions are necessary or likely necessary, and results are defined clearly enough to be confirmed | No vague results such as "improved capacity" without a definition | p. 177 |
| 2.3 | The ToC is evaluable: nested ToCs or a simplified ToC where the intervention is complex | Pathway-level ToCs, or a stated reason why one level is enough | pp. 177 to 181 |
| 2.4 | External influences are shown, and any causally linked to the package are flagged | External factors drawn and classified | p. 175 |
| 2.5 | The ToC draws on prior research where it exists, not only the programme team's view | Literature cited for key mechanisms | p. 177 |
| 2.6 | The ToC has a timeline | Expected timing for results | p. 183 |
| 2.7 | Contested links are identified: which parts of the ToC are disputed, by whom, and why | A note on contested and well-accepted links | BetterEvaluation, step 2 |

## Step 3. Existing evidence

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Existing evidence (monitoring data, prior evaluations, research) is gathered for results and for assumptions | Evidence mapped to links | p. 173 |
| 3.2 | Evidence on other influencing factors is gathered where the question requires it | Matches the question type in 1.2 | p. 176 |
| 3.3 | **(critical)** Implementation is verified: the intervention's activities were delivered as the ToC sets out | Evidence on what was delivered, to whom and when | BetterEvaluation, conditions for a sufficient story; p. 182 |

## Step 4. Assess the contribution claim and the challenges to it

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Each link is rated strong or weak, with reasons | A link-by-link assessment | BetterEvaluation, step 4 |
| 4.2 | Weak links are named as the place where new evidence is most needed | Data collection plan follows from 4.1 | BetterEvaluation, step 4 |
| 4.3 | Challenges to the claim are sought, not only support | Disconfirming evidence actively looked for | p. 173 |
| 4.4 | Who agrees and who disagrees with the story is recorded, with reasons, and disagreement is treated as a lead for more evidence | Named groups and their objections | BetterEvaluation, step 4 |

## Step 5. Additional evidence

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | New data collection targets the weak links, not the whole ToC evenly | Clear line from 4.2 to the instruments | BetterEvaluation, step 5 |
| 5.2 | More than one source verifies key links | Triangulation shown | Delahais and Toulemonde 2012, cited at p. 177 |
| 5.3 | An explicit causal inference method is used (e.g. process tracing tests, multiple lines and levels of evidence) | Method named and applied | BetterEvaluation, critical issues |

## Step 6. Revise and strengthen the claim

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | **(critical)** The ToC was verified: the pathway results and assumptions happened | Evidence for each link and assumption | p. 183, Table 1 tool 1 |
| 6.2 | Hoop tests passed: plausible time sequence, reasonable effort, expected effects seen | All three addressed | pp. 183 to 185 |
| 6.3 | **(critical)** A causal narrative explains how the package worked, beyond showing the ToC was realised | Mechanisms explained, barriers to change addressed | pp. 182 to 185 |
| 6.4 | Any claim that the intervention was a causal factor rests on more than hoop tests (e.g. a unique effect, a smoking gun) | Strong evidence for the causal role, or the claim is kept modest | p. 186 |
| 6.5 | The story was reviewed critically by someone outside the team (steering group, peer reviewer, stakeholders challenging it) | Review process described | BetterEvaluation example (Delahais and Toulemonde 2017) |

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | There is evidence of iteration: the ToC or the story changed in response to evidence | Changes described, with reasons | p. 173; BetterEvaluation, step 6 |

## The claim itself

| # | Check | What a pass looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| C.1 | **(critical)** The claim has both parts: it contributed, and how | A verdict plus a causal narrative, saying for whom where that matters | pp. 174 to 175, 182 |
| C.2 | **(critical)** The claim answers the question that was set | A contribution question ends in a contribution claim; a ranking or "main driver" claim appears only if the design set out to answer it | p. 176 |
| C.3 | Other factors are examined to the extent the question requires, no more and no less | Contribution question: outside factors only if causally linked. Causes-of-outcome question: explored | pp. 175 to 176 |
| C.4 | Factors labelled "rivals" are checked: do they undercut the claim, or work alongside the intervention? | Factors that work with the intervention are not presented as defeated rivals | p. 175 |
| C.5 | **(critical)** The claim does not rest on stakeholder opinion about the contribution | Opinions used for evidence on results and assumptions only | p. 175 |
| C.6 | No claim about the size or share of the contribution, unless a separate method supports it | No "60% due to the programme" from CA alone | pp. 181, 187 |
| C.7 | Any relative importance claim defines "importance" (perceived importance, role, funds, constraints addressed) | Definition stated | p. 181 |
| C.8 | Claims about other places or populations are argued: same outputs possible, assumptions hold, narrative still valid | Generalisation argued, or not claimed | p. 186 |
| C.9 | Confidence is stated, and matches the evidence | Strength words fit the tests passed | BetterEvaluation, overview |
| C.10 | Claims about distant outcomes rest on credible claims for the earlier links in the pathway | No impact claim without the intermediate links | p. 182 |
| C.11 | Methods are reported transparently: data sources, how informants were chosen, how evidence was weighed, and any departure from the steps | A reader could retrace the analysis | Buetti et al. 2025; Bohni Nielsen and Lemire 2026 |

## Five verification questions

After the checklist, answer these five in writing. Each one should be capable of exposing an error.

1. **Question fit.** If I read only the evaluation question and the final claim, does the claim answer that question, or a bigger one?
2. **Weakest link.** Which single causal link carries the most weight, and what is the strongest piece of evidence for it? Does more than one source support it?
3. **Other factors.** Which other factor would most embarrass the claim if it turned out to matter? Was it examined, and on what evidence?
4. **Opinion check.** Remove every statement where a stakeholder says the programme made a difference. Does the claim still stand?
5. **External check.** Pick one claim and trace it to the source document, dataset or transcript. Does the source say what the report says it says?

## Verdict

- **Credible CA**: no critical fails, few partials, claim matches question and evidence.
- **CA with a narrowed claim**: one or more critical fails that are fixed by narrowing the claim (for example, from "main driver" to "contributed, as follows").
- **Not a CA as reported**: weak or missing ToC (2.1), claim built on opinion (C.5), or no causal narrative (6.3). Mayne says studies resting only on opinion should carry a different label (p. 175), and that work on a weak ToC is not CA (p. 176).

If implementation fails (3.3), the honest finding is usually that the intervention could not have contributed as theorised; report why.
